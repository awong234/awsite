# They don't actually care about what they're talking about

## Clearly misunderstands the Jevons paradox

Oh look, [another lexicological AI fad.](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2025/02/04/g-s1-46018/ai-deepseek-economics-jevons-paradox) Can you say <a href="/posts/2026/08/the-final-resting-of-laurels#an-aside-on-taste"><span class='rainbow'>taste</span>?</a>

> DHH (01:11:53): It’s not clear what’s gonna happen at all. Some stats actually
show an increase in openings because the advent of AI is so dramatically
lowering the price of programs that people want a lot more programs. This is the
classic, the Jevons paradox, that says when the price of something goes down,
there’s gonna be more demand for it.

The [Jevons paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox) is not a
simple restating of supply and demand. It is not a paradox to observe that
demand might increase when prices fall; that much should be pretty obvious to
anyone having taken economics 101.

The paradox is that increasing _efficiency_ of systems do not tend to reduce
their overall consumption. It is originally and primarily cast in relation to
energy use and speaks to another problem in our future -- that of climate change
-- and what the Jevons paradox *actually* predicts is that without the proper
incentives, all the renewable energy in the world _will not_ undercut our total
emissions, deforestation, consumption of resources, etc; they will only serve to
fuel _more_ consumption. There will be no "green" AI if the Jevons paradox holds
true.

This is the real implication of the Jevons paradox that AI optimists blithely
paper over. AI won't solve climate change because we already have a solution:
consume a fuck-ton less resources.

The paradox is partly resolved by the Tragedy of the Commons, where an abundant
and freely available resource becomes abused by actors for their own selfish
ends, resulting in depletion of the common resource. Another angle of the
Tragedy is convenient externalization of negative effects, the pollution of
common areas because containment is too expensive.

But it is even when things are cheap -- not even free, just cheap -- that people
begin to over-use resources for increasingly less productive means. Making
access to resources cheaper and easier creates waste. It is _wasteful_ for me to
leave the light on in my basement all night, but we have a cat with eye damage
so I do it because LEDs let me do it cheaply. It is a _tragic waste_ to have
disposable _super-computers_ that we call smartphones -- many times the compute
needed to get us to the moon that first time -- with which we do _nothing of
note_ except dick around on social media.

Last month I misplaced my water bottle and had to use a plastic Smart Water
bottle during my workouts instead. I thought that if a person in the 17th
century came across a plastic water bottle -- a vessel that could not shatter,
could not rust or decay, was easily opened and closed, and water-tight -- that
they would count it a treasure and would be appalled if I told them I just
*threw it away* after finishing the water. Indeed it worked just fine for the
month it took to find my other water bottle; but cheap things are made cheaper
still when they cross the threshold into disposability.

What AI _will_ create is cheap code, cheap disposable code that nobody will want
to fix or maintain because *why would you* when just like a water bottle or
smartphone you can just throw it away and buy a replacement? Why would you have
consideration for the verdant and pacific fields of FOSS when you can instead
[spew slop you don't care about for bug
bounties](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)?

AI code will be the PFAS forever chemicals spewed into the environment to win
the convenience of *your eggs not sticking to your pan*.

## Clearly doesn't care about art

> Lex Fridman (01:28:16): I mean, this really feels… It’s– it’s hilarious and
awesome to watch and inspiring to watch. It’s like Picasso all of a sudden f–
like, getting access to image generation and getting super excited and switching
in a matter of months. That’s literally what happened. So, like, it’s an artist
that appreciated beautiful code, and all of a sudden you’re talking about having
fun-
>
> DHH (01:28:37) I think Picasso is actually a good parallel because if you look
at his early work where he learned his craft, it was painting realistic
paintings, right? Like, he was training to be a master of the old ways of
depicting reality the best way we knew how, and then along comes all these other
forms of expression. Cubism comes along.
>
> DHH (01:29:01) And all these o– forms of abstract expression, he’s not decrying
that he’s not doing Renaissance paintings anymore, that he’s not just painting
the perfect depiction of an apple. He’s reimagining the apple to be a freaking
square, and he’s getting excited about that. And I feel that transition, that
we’ve gone from this one mode of expression, and we’ve suddenly opened the gates
where the, like, the color spectrum is so much wider. I’m seeing more.

It's incredible that they would expect any artist to be "excited" by image
generation. I somehow doubt it's a popular path for artists out there who have
mastered their craft to abandon it all for generated products.

They consider Picasso at the height of his career, once again understanding this
situation through the lens of someone who has _already_ accumulated their
capital. If image generation arose just as Picasso picked up the brush for the
first time and he was so excited as they think he would be, would there be a
Picasso? Would there be cubism if Picasso didn't help develop it?

Or would he have *actually* now truly been stuck in a loop of Renaissance
realism, in awe of the output as something seemingly far beyond his nascent
capability to reproduce and so never pushing himself beyond what was already in
vogue and could be simply generated without effort?

Even if we grant that Picasso could speak all of his art into existence does
anyone _really_ believe that is the medium he would thrive in, that any artist
would thrive in? No, I think most assuredly Picasso would have rejected
generation as a means of expressing himself.

The thing about science fiction is that it's not _really_ in the business of
predicting the future, it's in the business of examining our species' ability to
cope with the future. Prediction is incidental. Science fiction cares about
commenting on our current society _today_ more than it does about simply
imagining the future. If they understood any of that then they'd understand that
science fiction shows more than just a vision of the future, it warns of the
consequences those visions have. And there is hardly a story about AI that has
nice consequences.
